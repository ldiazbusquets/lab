Classic machine learning

Before chatbots, machine learning was already approving loans, flagging fraud and sorting customers, and it still does most of that work today. This page trains the classic methods live on a set of loan applicants, so you can see exactly how each one decides.

01Learning from examplesFeatures and labels

Show a model past loans and how they turned out. It learns to judge new ones.

Past loan applicantsHover a dot
RepaidDefaultedTest set (hidden from training)

Each dot is a past applicant, placed by two facts the lender knew at the time: their credit score (across) and their debt-to-income ratio, the share of monthly income already going to debt payments (up).

Those inputs are called features. What actually happened, repaid or defaulted, is the label. Learning from labelled examples like this is called supervised learning, and it is how most business AI works.

The test set

Some applicants are held back and never shown to the models. Turn on Show test set to see them. Every score on this page is measured on those unseen people, because, as module 01 showed, doing well on data you trained on proves very little.

Applicants are generated example data, not real people. Real credit models use many more features.

02Decision treesTwenty questions

A decision tree asks yes-or-no questions, one after another.

Tree splitting the applicants
Depth
The rules it learned

    The tree looks for the single question that best separates repaid from defaulted, such as "is the credit score below 640?". Then it does the same again inside each half, and again, building a flowchart.

    The depth is how many questions it may ask in a row. Shaded blue areas get approved; red areas get denied. Below the chart are the exact rules, in plain language.

    Watch the score

    Raise the depth and the tree starts carving tiny boxes around individual dots. Training accuracy keeps climbing, but test accuracy stops improving and usually drops. It is the same overfitting you saw in module 01.

    Why lenders like trees

    Every decision can be explained as a short list of rules. That matters: in the US, a lender that turns someone down must tell them the specific reasons.

    03Nearest neighboursJudged by the company you keep

    Find the most similar past applicants, and go with what happened to them.

    Click to place a new applicant
    Neighbours
    Click anywhere on the chart to place a new applicant.

    Nearest neighbours barely learns anything up front. When a new applicant arrives, it finds the past applicants most similar to them and lets them vote. If most of the 9 closest people repaid, approve.

    Click the chart to drop in an applicant and see who their neighbours are. The shading shows what it would decide anywhere on the map.

    Choosing how many neighbours

    With 1 neighbour, the map turns patchy: one unlucky dot can flip a whole area. With 41 it gets smooth but starts ignoring real patterns. Somewhere in between scores best on the test set.

    A practical catch

    "Similar" needs a sense of distance. A credit score runs into the hundreds while debt-to-income is a percentage, so the inputs have to be rescaled to the same range first, or the score would drown out everything else. This page does that for you.

    04ClusteringNo labels at all

    Sometimes there is no right answer to learn from. Clustering finds the groups on its own.

    Brokerage clients
    Groups

    These are example brokerage clients, placed by how often they trade (across) and how large their account is (up). Nobody has labelled them. The goal is just to find natural groups, called unsupervised learning.

    k-means drops in a few group centres (the crosses), then repeats two moves: give every client to the nearest centre, then move each centre to the middle of its clients. Press One step to watch each move, or Run.

    How many groups?

    You choose. The chart below shows how tightly packed the groups are for each choice. It always improves with more groups, so look for the elbow, where adding another stops helping much.

    Press New start a few times with 4 or 5 groups: different starting spots can settle on different answers. Real tools run it many times and keep the best.

    Where it is used

    Customer segmentation: deciding which clients get which service model, which products to pitch, and which accounts look unusual.

    What the industry uses

    For data in rows and columns, like loan files, transactions and account records, the strongest methods today are still built from trees: random forests and their stronger cousin, gradient-boosted trees. Deep learning wins on images, sound and language; trees usually win on spreadsheets.

    On this small example, with only two features and a fairly straight boundary, simple logistic regression often comes out on top. Trees pull ahead on real files with dozens of features that interact in messy ways.

    The trade-off

    The most accurate model is not always the one a bank can use. Regulators expect lenders to explain decisions and show they are fair, so a slightly weaker model that can be explained often beats a stronger black box.
